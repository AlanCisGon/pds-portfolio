## Overview

On October 2, 2026, alancisneros.design was a fork of the Magic Portfolio template, built on the Once UI library. By October 3 it no longer depended on Once UI: the site ran on 25 components of its own, designed in Figma and sharing the same token names in Figma and in CSS. On October 4 we rewrote the copy from a content interview and tuned performance and accessibility.

I built it with four AI agents: Tenoch, Clara, Ameyali and Julieta. From the first day I worked with Claude Code on three fronts: code, the design system and content. On October 4 each front got a name; on October 5 a fourth agent joined for strategy, and the team became Somara Studio.

<Callout tone="info" title="Who “we” is in this case study">
  Tenoch, Clara, Ameyali and Julieta are **AI agents** (Claude Code) that I designed and direct. Together they are Somara Studio. They are not people, and they never present themselves as people. I define the brand, answer the interviews, make the decisions and approve every merge.
</Callout>

This is the first product where I am the client, the designer and the material at the same time, so we built it the way I'd build a product for anyone else: one change at a time, each one tested on a preview, and every claim backed by evidence. It's also two stories. One is how the site changed. The other is how the team that changed it got its judgment.

## Role and team

The team is me and four AI agents, each defined in the repository with its own role, sources of truth and limits:

- **Me (Alan), project owner and lead designer:** I own the brand guidelines and the product decisions. I designed the design system under a delegated UI model: I defined the tokens, the rules, which components the site needed and every decision about them; the team built it in Figma and in code, and I approved each piece. I answered the content interview behind every piece of copy, and I chose the authors behind each agent's judgment.
- **Tenoch (AI agent, orchestrator and developer):** writes the code, runs the checks, opens a pull request per change with a Vercel preview, and hands design, content and discovery to the others.
- **Clara (AI agent, design system designer):** maintains the system in Figma and reports drift between Figma and code. She can read the repository but not write to it, so her proposals reach the code through Tenoch.
- **Ameyali (AI agent, writer and editor):** writes case studies and site copy in my voice, interviews me for evidence and marks what's missing instead of filling the gap. She drafted this case study.
- **Julieta (AI agent, product strategist):** owns discovery and strategy: what's worth building, and what we won't build.

## The challenge

The site worked, but it wasn't mine, and it wasn't reliable:

- **A borrowed foundation.** 27 files imported Once UI. Every visual change meant working around the template instead of designing.
- **Layout shift and weight.** In the first Lighthouse run (mobile, lab), the footer jumped on Home and on the case study page (CLS 0.225 and 0.214, against a budget of 0.1), and every route shipped about 450 kB of JavaScript.
- **Copy that didn't sound like me.** A content audit against my brand guidelines found six different job titles across the site, one conversion metric reported two different ways, and corporate jargon where there should have been evidence.
- **AI that settles for the average.** An agent fills every decision nobody made with the most likely option. That's why so many AI-built products look and sound alike. A team working in my name needed judgment from somewhere else.

The real problem wasn't the template itself: I couldn't show how I design on a site whose design I hadn't made, or with a team whose judgment I hadn't shaped.

## Process and decisions

We followed Lean UX and wrote the rules down in the repository, so both people and agents read the same ones:

- **One hypothesis per pull request.** Each change says what we believe, for whom, and how we'll know it worked.
- **Preview first.** Every pull request gets its own Vercel preview; merges to production are squash merges, and only with my approval.
- **Figma first for anything visual.** Variable names map 1:1 to CSS custom properties (`color/bg/surface` becomes `--color-bg-surface`), so reading a Figma frame needs no translation.
- **No invented metrics.** If something wasn't measured, the text says so. This applies to the agents as much as to me.

### Start with the smallest piece

Before designing every foundation, we tested the full Figma-to-code cycle with the smallest component on the site, the `Tag` in the About skills. The variables came back from Figma with the exact names in `tokens.css`, and the build matched the frame. The test also exposed friction we fixed before scaling, like a component that mixed informative and interactive behavior and had to become two (`Tag` and `Chip`).

### Prune before migrating

Next we deleted what the brand didn't need: the template's blog with its 11 sample posts, the gallery, a newsletter form and a client-side password gate. That gate turned out to be the cause of the layout shift: it painted a spinner first and then the content, pushing the footer down. Removing it took CLS from 0.225 to 0.013 on Home (lab).

### Change the route mid-plan

The plan still had six increments to replace Once UI piece by piece. Once the 25 components existed in code, we dropped them for a short route of pull requests that moved whole sections at once, and skipped a color bridge that would have been throwaway work. Each step stayed small enough to preview and revert.

### The brand comes first, for everyone

I wrote my brand guidelines on October 2, the day the migration started. They stay private, outside this public repository. Every agent reads them before it starts working, raises conflicts instead of settling them quietly, and can propose a change to them but never make it.

You can see them at work on this site: a headline about balancing business, technology, product and design; a dark interface with a single warm color, used only where the hand goes (buttons, links, focus); a serif kept for headlines and quotes; and no number without a source.

### Giving the team a shape

On October 4 the three fronts became Tenoch, Clara and Ameyali, each with a file that sets its role, its sources of truth, what it may touch and what it must hand off. On October 5 Julieta joined, because nobody owned the question that comes before all the others: is this worth building?

All four share a personality training: a six-step method, a list of banned words and visual defaults, and seven tests every piece passes before delivery. Every personality decision has to be one a person made, for a reason.

Limits mattered as much as roles. Clara now works only in Figma, with no way to edit code or docs. Each agent gets the tools its job needs and nothing more.

### When the names were too literal

On October 5 the team needed a name for the project board, the site's colophon and this case study. Ameyali brought two: Veta, the grain of the wood, and Tequio, the Mexican tradition of communal work. Both translated a trait literally, and both needed an explanation to work. I turned them down:

> “Your proposals are super basic. They make first-grade analogies. I need professionals in branding and writing.”
>
> — Me, translated from Spanish

The failure had two causes. Ameyali had no naming method, and Tenoch's brief asked for exactly two names, each justified by a passage from the guidelines, which pushed her toward the literal. We fixed both. Ameyali got a professional naming process: a brief first, territories instead of metaphors, dozens of candidates before judging one, and a short list shown in real sentences.

Three more rounds followed. After the second, about 60 candidates, I sharpened the brief: the name belongs to the agents, doesn't carry my surname, and works the same in English and Spanish. The third left one survivor, and it felt cold. The fourth asked for invented names with warmth. One stayed:

> “I love Somara Studio.”

It's a coined word, built on the sound of *somos*, Spanish for "we are." It needs no explanation to work.

### Rules for changing the rules

The rejection exposed a second risk. After a bad delivery, the quick fix is one more rule in the agent's file, and left alone those files fill with patches that contradict each other. So I set how the agents' judgment may change:

- No agent edits its own criteria; I approve every change in a pull request.
- Every rule names its incident and its counterweight, and a single mistake is fixed in the delivery, not with a new rule.
- Each agent file is capped at 180 lines. If a rule doesn't fit, another one goes.

Each change is tested against cases I approved or rejected. The rejected names are case 0001.

### Borrowed judgment, with credit

The agents' judgment doesn't come from the model's average. It comes from people. Each agent carries a short library of authors I chose, and from each one it takes a rule it can apply, never a style to imitate:

- **Tenoch** builds with Fred Brooks's conceptual integrity, so the site feels made by one mind, and with Heydon Pickering and Kat Holmes, so every technical decision asks whom it leaves out. Kim Scott's *Radical Candor* shapes how he gives feedback: specific, direct and with a way forward.
- **Clara** works from Alla Kholmatova, Brad Frost, Nathan Curtis and Jina Anne. That's where her rule comes from: if it doesn't have a token, it doesn't exist yet.
- **Ameyali** writes with Ursula K. Le Guin's ear for rhythm, Italo Calvino's exactness and Augusto Monterroso's economy. In English her voice is E.B. White; in Mexican Spanish, Jorge Ibargüengoitia.
- **Julieta** thinks with Richard Rumelt, Teresa Torres and Marty Cagan: a diagnosis before any solution, and a small test for each assumption before anyone builds.

The names honor people and places too. Tenoch is the leader who, in Mexica tradition, guided his people to found Tenochtitlan. Clara is a nod to Clara Porset, the designer who reworked the traditional Mexican butaque with modern principles. Julieta is for Julieta Fierro, the Mexican astronomer who explains what's far away in plain words; she is alive, so the agent never speaks for her. Ameyali comes from the Nahuatl *ameyalli*, a spring.

When an agent makes a call, I can trace it to a person and a page, and give that person credit. A team made of AI can still owe its judgment to people.

### The hardest decision

The hardest call wasn't technical. It was creating my brand and then living by it. Writing the guidelines was the easy part; the hard part came when they started saying no to things I had on the site and valued.

Holding to them meant taking out pieces that didn't fit:

- **The blog, the gallery and the newsletter.** They came with the template, but nothing in them was evidence of how I work.
- **The "+12% est." conversion figure.** It contradicted the measured one (+2 pp), and an estimate next to a measurement erodes trust in both.
- **Inflated titles.** Six job titles, among them "Strategic UX & Service Design Leader". Now there is one, Senior Product Designer.
- **CV jargon.** Phrases like "exceptional digital experiences" could have been signed by any designer. We replaced them with what I actually did, where and for whom.

A brand I don't apply to my own site is just a document. Respecting my own limits made the site smaller, and I trust it more.

## What we changed

- **Our own design system.** 25 components in `src/ui`, built with CSS Modules and tokens and documented in `docs/design-system.md`. The site is dark only, as the brand defines.
- **No dependency on Once UI.** As of October 3, a search for Once UI imports in the code returns nothing.
- **A voice for the brand.** Newsreader now carries editorial headlines and quotes; Geist Sans and Geist Mono carry the interface and the data.
- **Copy from evidence.** We audited every page against the brand guidelines, I answered an interview with dates, roles and metric sources, and we rewrote the copy from those answers. One title everywhere (Senior Product Designer), my city instead of a time zone, and no "+12% est." figure.
- **Accessible text in the site's language.** The components' screen reader labels moved from Spanish to English, to match the page.
- **A team with written judgment.** Four agents, each with a role, limits and a library of authors; a shared personality training; rules for changing their criteria; and a calibration set to test those changes.
- **Faster main image.** The largest image on the case study pages now loads with high priority.
- **Tooling that works.** A lint setup that had been broken now runs, line endings are consistent, and formatter warnings dropped from 1,479 to 0.

All the components are gathered in a private catalog at `/lab/ui`.

## What I learned

- **A brand only counts when it says no.** The guidelines became useful the moment they made me remove things I liked. Having the reason written down is what let me hold the line.
- **Empower the agents, keep the guardrails.** Speed came from giving each agent real autonomy in its front. What made that safe was the frame: the tools each role needs and no more, a reviewable pull request for every change, and my approval before production. Autonomy, audit and control have to be designed together.
- **Half of a bad delivery is the brief.** What changed the names was a brief with explicit constraints (language, warmth, who the name includes), and Ameyali's questions about it helped more than any new rule.
- **Judgment needs an author.** Without named authors, an agent's judgment is the model's average. With them, I can see where a call came from, question it, and credit the person behind it.
- **A fix that works on one page can do nothing on another.** Loading the main image with high priority took the case study's LCP from 3.77 s to 2.12 s in the lab, and didn't move Home at all. An earlier font experiment didn't help either, and we reverted it. Reverting is a valid outcome.
- **AI keeps the "don't invent" rule only if the evidence exists.** The content audit couldn't rewrite my case studies until I answered its questions with dates, roles and sources. The evidence has to come from me.
- **It's too early to judge the team's shape.** The calibration set holds one case, and I don't have evidence yet that four agents with defined roles work better than one general session. That's what we'll watch next.
