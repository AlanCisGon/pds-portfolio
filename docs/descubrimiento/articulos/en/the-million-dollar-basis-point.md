# **The million-dollar basis point in e-commerce**

June 2, 2026  
Alan Cisneros Gonzalez  
*Edited by Ameyali*

I wrote this for the designers on my team. When they kick off a project, they fill out a one-pager with experience, conversion, and business metrics, and they kept asking me the same thing: how do I know if a metric is moving the right way or the wrong way? When should I worry, and when can I call a winning trend? To answer that, you first need to know what a basis point means. This is the example I wrote for them.

As product designers, we often struggle to show, in actual numbers, how much we contribute to the business. But there's a tool on our side. We just have to ask:

**What is a conversion rate, what on earth is a basis point, how does it turn into money, and why does it matter for product design?**

Here's the simplest way to picture it. Imagine you're standing at the entrance of a huge department store, the kind that sells a bit of everything.  
Thousands of people walk through the door every day. Some know exactly what they came for. Many others just want to browse the clothes, test the mattresses, people-watch, or enjoy the air conditioning.  
Online, each of those visits to the website or the app is a **session**, and together they make up your **traffic**. Your **conversion rate** is the percentage of all those people who actually take out their wallet, pay, and walk out with a receipt.

### **What's a basis point of conversion?**

Online traffic is enormous (we're talking millions of visits a week), so conversion rates tend to be small numbers. According to Dynamic Yield's benchmark[^1], the average e-commerce conversion rate in the Americas is 2.59% over the past twelve months. A store that sells across many categories can fall below that average. For this article, let's imagine one that converts at **0.80%**. Working with decimals as small as 0.81%, 0.82%, or 0.75% gets confusing fast.

That's why digital analytics uses the **basis point** (often written *bps*).

The rule is simple:

* **One full percent** (also called a percentage point) equals **100 basis points**.  
* So **1 basis point equals 0.01%**, one hundredth of one percent.

With that in mind:

- If your conversion rate goes from **0.80% to 0.81%**, congratulations: **you gained 1 basis point**.
- If it goes from **0.80% to 0.90%**, **you gained 10 basis points**.
- And if you double it, from **0.80% to 1.60%**, **you gained 80 basis points**.

Back at the store: if 80 out of every 10,000 people who walk in today buy something, your conversion rate is 0.80%. If you win over **just one more person** out of those 10,000, now 81 buy. Your conversion rate just rose to 0.81%. **That one extra person is your basis point.**

## **How does that basis point turn into real money?**

In a physical store, getting one extra person to buy a \$30 pair of pants doesn't change anyone's life. But online, where it's not 10,000 people walking in but **millions every week**, that tiny basis point multiplies fast.  
Here's the formula for what one basis point is worth at the register:

```
Value of 1 basis point ($) = Sessions (traffic) × 0.0001 × Average order value ($)
```

*Keep in mind that this is simple proportion. It's for **sizing the impact of your work**, not a revenue promise.*

Let's do the math. Here's what **a single basis point (0.01%)** is worth in three hypothetical scenarios for our imaginary store. The numbers are round on purpose: they're for practicing the calculation, not describing any real company.

### **Scenario 1: a typical week**

1. **Visits (traffic):** 2 million sessions.  
2. **Average order value:** \$80.  
3. **How do I size 1 basis point?**  
   Multiply 2,000,000 visits by 0.0001 (the basis point).  
   That gives you **200 extra orders**.  
4. Multiply those 200 orders by the \$80 average order value.  
5. **Result:** **\$16,000 extra in a single week.**

Keep that one-basis-point gain going all year, and after 52 weeks you'll have added **\$832,000** in revenue. *(The math: \$16,000 × 52.)*

### **Scenario 2: a high-demand week**

1. **Visits (traffic):** 2.5 million sessions.  
2. **Average order value:** \$75.  
3. **How do I size 1 basis point?**  
   2,500,000 visits × 0.0001 = **250 extra orders**.  
4. 250 orders × \$75 = \$18,750.  
5. **Result:** **\$18,750 in recovered sales that week.**

### **Scenario 3: the big sale week**

1. **Visits (traffic):** 4 million sessions.  
2. **Average order value:** \$78.  
3. **How do I size 1 basis point?**  
   4,000,000 visits × 0.0001 = **400 extra orders**.  
4. 400 orders × \$78 = \$31,200.  
5. **Result:** **\$31,200 in a single week.**

During big events, traffic doubles, so **a single basis point is worth almost twice as much**: \$31,200 versus \$16,000 in a typical week.

## Why this matters for your product design strategy

When design or engineering teams pitch projects like *"let's make the app faster,"* *"let's redesign the payment methods screen,"* or *"let's move the credit calculator out of checkout,"* finance leaders sometimes hesitate, because it sounds like cosmetic work.

Knowing what a basis point is worth changes the conversation:

* Say your team makes the **payment methods** screen a little clearer and easier to understand, and that cuts cart abandonment just enough to gain **10 basis points** (from 0.80% to 0.90%)...
* In a typical week like Scenario 1, those 10 basis points are worth **\$160,000 extra a week**: 2,000 extra orders × \$80. Held all year, that's **\$8.32 million**.

With those numbers in hand, it's clear that an interface improvement, however small it looks, isn't a decorative expense. It can be one of the most profitable investments a company makes in its digital channel.
